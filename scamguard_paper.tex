% ─── Abstract (Single Contiguous Paragraph for IEEEtran) ───────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Mobile short message service phishing (smishing) and fraudulent hyperlink redirection represent a rapidly escalating threat to consumer digital privacy, personal credentials, and banking security. While contemporary cloud-hosted Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate impressive semantic reasoning, deploying monolithic foundation models for real-time mobile traffic inspection introduces prohibitive per-request latency, recurring operational API expenditure, and severe privacy exposure. In this work, we present \textbf{ScamGuard}, a fully local, reproducible screening baseline engineered for high-throughput edge inspection without reliance on external cloud inference. ScamGuard decouples incoming message inspection into two specialized processing branches: (1) a natural language text discriminator pairing sublinear word-level Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) n-gram representations with class-balanced Logistic Regression, and (2) a static hyperlink discriminator integrating 13 lexical, structural, and information-theoretic domain features with character-level subword TF-IDF representations powered by Histogram-Based Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (HistGradientBoosting). To effectively reconcile textual social engineering narratives with embedded hyperlink reputation without generating synthetic pair artifacts, we formalize a logistic meta-learner incorporating an explicit second-order interaction cross-product. We train and rigorously benchmark ScamGuard across large-scale international English smishing corpora (including Mendeley SMS Phishing and the USENIX SOUPS SmishX dataset) alongside a 549,346-record global phishing URL repository, enforcing strict zero-leakage grouped holdouts based on Public Suffix List (PSL) registrable domains and normalized text hashing. On an untouched test holdout of 14,799 SMS messages, the text branch achieves an Accuracy of \textbf{93.82\%}, an F1-score of \textbf{0.9055}, and a ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9892}. On a disjoint 9,996-sample external URL holdout, the hyperlink branch achieves an Accuracy of \textbf{92.11\%}, an F1-score of \textbf{0.8031}, and a ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9627}. On 2,632 real paired messages containing live URLs, the interaction stacking meta-learner attains an F1-score of \textbf{0.9299}, outperforming single-branch baselines and conventional heuristic max-fusion while operating entirely on local CPU hardware with sub-50 millisecond inference latency.
\end{abstract}

% ─── Section 1: Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
Social engineering scams delivered via Short Message Service (SMS), collectively designated as \emph{smishing}, and deceptive hyperlink redirection constitute the predominant entry vector for unauthorized financial transfers, credential harvesting, and identity theft globally \cite{apwg2025}. According to industry surveillance reports, losses resulting from mobile telecommunication impersonation fraud exceeded billions of dollars globally in 2024 alone \cite{apwg2025}. Threat syndicates leverage sophisticated psychological exploitation techniques---most notably manufactured urgency, banking suspension alerts, parcel delivery failures, and lottery notifications---to coerce victims into clicking embedded links leading to deceptive landing portals.

Recently, the computer security research community has explored Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to analyze suspicious communications \cite{berjawi2026, shaikh2026}. While foundation models provide compelling qualitative reasoning, relying exclusively on remote, cloud-hosted neural architectures for real-time endpoint traffic inspection encounters several critical operational barriers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency \& Computational Overhead:} Offloading individual SMS notifications or browsing activities to cloud APIs incurs hundreds of milliseconds of round-trip network transmission overhead and unsustainable recurring token expenses.
    \item \textbf{Privacy Infringement \& Regulatory Constraints:} Transmitting private consumer telecommunications to third-party commercial cloud endpoints creates substantial regulatory and ethical liabilities under modern data sovereignty mandates (e.g., GDPR).
    \item \textbf{Data Contamination and Synthetic Leakage:} A concerning proportion of existing academic smishing literature relies on randomly split evaluation partitions, where identical scam templates or shared domain hostnames span across both training and test subsets, artificially inflating reported empirical performance. Furthermore, many multimodal baselines construct synthetic text-URL pairs, introducing unnatural artifacts that fail to reflect real-world campaign distributions.
\end{enumerate}

To address these challenges, we introduce \textbf{ScamGuard}, an edge-oriented, fully offline screening framework designed for rapid, reproducible mobile scam triage. ScamGuard adopts an asymmetric dual-branch topology: a word-level linear classifier modeling social engineering linguistic cues, and a gradient-boosted decision tree pipeline modeling URL structural topology and character distributions. Base branch probabilities are unified through a second-order logistic stacking meta-learner trained strictly on real paired messages.

\textbf{Core Contributions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproducible Zero-Leakage Evaluation Protocol:} We introduce a verifiable grouping framework combining the official Mozilla Public Suffix List (PSL) with canonical text hashing, proving strict mutual exclusivity between training, validation, and holdout splits across both message templates and domain identities.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Dual-Branch Feature Engineering:} We deploy a sublinear word-level TF-IDF representation with class-balanced Logistic Regression for natural text, alongside a unified 13-feature lexical-structural and character n-gram GBDT model for hyperlink inspection.
    \item \textbf{Interaction Stacking Meta-Fusion:} We formalize and evaluate a second-order logistic meta-learner that models the synergistic co-occurrence of manipulative text narratives and deceptive hyperlink features, outperforming heuristic max-pooling and linear stacking baselines.
    \item \textbf{Verifiable Real-World Benchmarks:} We validate our framework on an untouched holdout of 14,799 SMS messages, 9,996 external URLs, 2,632 real paired messages, and the independent USENIX SOUPS SmishX human-annotated test set.
\end{itemize}

% ─── Section 2: Related Work ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Smishing and Text-Based Social Engineering Detection}
Early automated smishing detection systems relied heavily on deterministic regular expressions, keyword blacklists, and domain reputation lookups \cite{cormack2007}. While computationally lightweight, static signature tables fail against simple token permutations, homoglyphs, and dynamic pretext narratives. Classical machine learning pipelines using bag-of-words and n-gram representations with linear classifiers demonstrated strong baseline utility \cite{pedregosa2011}. Recent work by Berjawi \emph{et al.} \cite{berjawi2026} proposed adaptive uncertainty routing (AURA) for enterprise email threat filtering, highlighting the necessity of selective escalation policies when automated classifiers operate near their decision boundary.

\subsection{Static and Structural Hyperlink Analysis}
Hyperlink security analysis generally branches into dynamic content rendering (e.g., headless browser execution and DOM tree inspection \cite{shaikh2026}) and static lexical-structural feature evaluation \cite{friedman2001}. Static feature extraction analyzes structural dimensions, path depth, character distributions, and information-theoretic indicators such as Shannon entropy \cite{shannon1948}. Character-level subword TF-IDF representations have emerged as an effective defense against typo-squatting, punycode substitutions, and obfuscated subdirectories without requiring live network roundtrips to retrieve malicious payloads.

% ─── Section 3: System Architecture and Methodology ───────────────────────────
\section{System Architecture and Methodology}
The ScamGuard processing pipeline operates across three interconnected stages: (1) Deterministic Preprocessing and Entity Canonicalization, (2) Specialized Dual-Branch Feature Extraction, and (3) Interaction Stacking Meta-Fusion.

\subsection{Deterministic Preprocessing and Entity Canonicalization}
To guarantee identical representations across training and local runtime inference, raw input strings undergo a deterministic 5-stage transformation pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unicode Normalization:} Raw Unicode text is normalized to Form C (NFC), resolving composite accents and character encodings.
    \item \textbf{Whitespace and Casing Harmonization:} Trailing, leading, and redundant internal whitespace characters are collapsed, and casing is harmonized for lexical tokenizers.
    \item \textbf{High-Variance Entity Masking:} Highly volatile tokens that introduce sparse vocabulary noise are replaced with canonical tokens: international phone numbers $\to \texttt{[PHONE]}$, monetary amounts $\to \texttt{[MONEY]}$, and verification credentials $\to \texttt{[OTP]}$.
    \item \textbf{Hyperlink Decoupling and Extraction:} Uniform Resource Identifiers (RFC 3986) are extracted via regular expressions. Hyperlinks are sanitized and passed to Branch 2, while the originating text retains the canonical token $\texttt{[URL]}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Branch 1: Word-Level Text Discriminator}
The text classification branch captures verbal manipulation, artificial urgency, and financial coercion. Preprocessed text streams are transformed using word-level Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) spanning unigrams and bigrams:
\begin{equation}
\text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \left(1 + \ln(\text{tf}(t, d))\right) \times \ln\left(\frac{1 + |D|}{1 + |\{d \in D : t \in d\}|}\right) + 1
\end{equation}
Sublinear term frequency scaling is applied to mitigate the influence of repetitive spam keywords. The vocabulary is bounded to the top 10,000 features under $L_2$ vector normalization. 

The feature space is classified using class-balanced Logistic Regression with $L_2$ regularization:
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{text}}(x) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \phi_{\text{text}}(x) + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\mathbf{w}^T \phi_{\text{text}}(x) + b)}}
\end{equation}
where inverse class weighting offsets the natural class imbalance inherent in real-world messaging traffic.

\subsection{Branch 2: Lexical-Structural URL Discriminator}
The URL screening branch processes extracted hyperlinks through a hybrid feature space combining 13 numerical lexical and structural features with character subword n-grams:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural Metrics:} String length $|u|$, hostname length, path length, query parameter count, and subdirectory slash depth.
    \item \textbf{Character Distributions:} Frequency counts of delimiter periods (\texttt{.}), hyphens (\texttt{-}), underscores (\texttt{\_}), ampersands (\texttt{\&}), and numeric digit ratios.
    \item \textbf{Security Indicators:} Binary indicators identifying raw IPv4 host addresses and non-standard web ports.
    \item \textbf{Shannon Information Entropy:} Quantifying algorithmic character randomness and domain generation algorithms (DGA) within the fully qualified domain name:
    \begin{equation}
    H(S) = -\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(c_i) \log_2 P(c_i)
    \end{equation}
    where $P(c_i)$ represents the empirical probability of character $c_i$ across unique characters in domain string $S$.
\end{enumerate}

The 13 continuous features are standardized via z-score normalization and concatenated with character n-gram TF-IDF representations (3--5 character window, capped at 1,000 dimensions) to capture brand spoofing patterns (e.g., \texttt{secure-paypa1}). The unified vector is classified using Histogram-Based Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (HistGradientBoosting) configured with a tree depth of 4 and 100 boosting iterations. For communications containing multiple embedded hyperlinks, the branch outputs the maximum threat probability: $p_{\text{url}} = \max_{u \in U} P(y=1|u)$.

\subsection{Interaction Stacking Meta-Learner}
When an incoming communication contains both textual prose and one or more hyperlinks, combining independent branch probabilities requires capturing super-additive threat interactions. We formalize a second-order logistic meta-learner:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{meta}} = 100 \times \sigma\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 p_{\text{text}} + \beta_2 p_{\text{url}} + \beta_3 (p_{\text{text}} \cdot p_{\text{url}})\right)
\label{eq:meta_stacking}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{text}}$ and $p_{\text{url}}$ are base estimator probabilities, and $(p_{\text{text}} \cdot p_{\text{url}})$ represents the multiplicative interaction cross-product modeling synergistic risk escalation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ScamGuard Dual-Branch Inference Pipeline}
\label{alg:inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Raw input message $M$, optional URL string $U_{\text{raw}}$, models $\mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{url}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{meta}}$
\STATE $T_{\text{norm}}, U_{\text{extracted}} \leftarrow \text{PreprocessAndExtract}(M, U_{\text{raw}})$
\IF{$U_{\text{extracted}} \neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $p_{\text{text}} \leftarrow \mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}.\text{predict\_proba}(T_{\text{norm}})$
    \STATE $p_{\text{url}} \leftarrow \max_{u \in U_{\text{extracted}}} \mathcal{M}_{\text{url}}.\text{predict\_proba}(u)$
    \STATE $\mathbf{z} \leftarrow [p_{\text{text}}, p_{\text{url}}, p_{\text{text}} \cdot p_{\text{url}}]$
    \STATE $s \leftarrow 100 \times \mathcal{M}_{\text{meta}}.\text{predict\_proba}(\mathbf{z})$
    \STATE $\text{mode} \leftarrow \text{``paired\_fusion''}$
\ELSIF{$T_{\text{norm}} \neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $p_{\text{text}} \leftarrow \mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}.\text{predict\_proba}(T_{\text{norm}})$
    \STATE $s \leftarrow 100 \times p_{\text{text}}, \quad \text{mode} \leftarrow \text{``text\_only''}$
\ELSE
    \STATE $p_{\text{url}} \leftarrow \max_{u \in U_{\text{extracted}}} \mathcal{M}_{\text{url}}.\text{predict\_proba}(u)$
    \STATE $s \leftarrow 100 \times p_{\text{url}}, \quad \text{mode} \leftarrow \text{``url\_only''}$
\ENDIF
\IF{$s \ge 80$}
    \STATE $\text{Decision} \leftarrow \text{BLOCK (High Risk)}$
\ELSIF{$s < 30$}
    \STATE $\text{Decision} \leftarrow \text{CLEAR (Benign)}$
\ELSE
    \STATE $\text{Decision} \leftarrow \text{REVIEW (Ambiguous Triage Window)}$
\ENDIF
\RETURN Score $s$, Decision, Top Signals, Mode
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Section 4: Data Provenance and Leakage Controls ──────────────────────────
\section{Data Provenance and Leakage Controls}

\subsection{Corpus Curation and Provenance}
We construct and evaluate ScamGuard using three verified corpora:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global Smishing Benchmark Corpus:} Sourced from the Combined Labeled Smishing Dataset (pinned at commit \texttt{3bf9983}), containing 84,863 raw entries. Quality screening and deduplication yield 73,989 clean records (51,428 benign, 22,561 smishing), including 8,995 real paired messages containing live URLs.
    \item \textbf{USENIX SOUPS SmishX Dataset:} Comprising multi-modal smishing samples published at SOUPS 2025 \cite{soups2025}, annotated under a two-researcher manual agreement protocol.
    \item \textbf{External Phishing URL Corpus:} A global dataset of 549,346 verified URLs. After canonical deduplication and excluding all hostnames present in the SMS corpus, a balanced benchmark of 50,000 URLs is retained.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Grouping Protocols to Prevent Data Leakage}
Data leakage across training and test partitions represents a severe vulnerability in published literature. To guarantee statistical independence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Normalized Text Equivalence Partitioning:} Messages sharing identical normalized lexical content are bound into atomic clusters.
    \item \textbf{Public Suffix List (PSL) Domain Clustering:} Hyperlinks sharing identical registrable second-level domains (e.g., \texttt{login.bank.com} and \texttt{verify.bank.com}) are grouped into single units using the official Mozilla Public Suffix List.
    \item \textbf{SMS-URL Mutual Exclusion:} All hostnames and registrable domains present within the SMS corpus are strictly barred from appearing within the external URL training split, ensuring the URL branch acts as a genuine external holdout.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Fold (OOF) Stacking:} The meta-learner is trained exclusively on out-of-fold probability vectors generated via 5-fold cross-validation strictly inside the training partition, preventing optimistic bias in meta-learner fitting.
\end{enumerate}

The experimental findings reveal critical system behaviors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Failure of Isolated URL Inspection:} The URL branch in isolation achieves an F1-score of only 0.8207 on paired messages. Attackers routinely disguise phishing links behind URL shorteners (e.g., \texttt{bit.ly}, \texttt{tinyurl}) or recently acquired non-blacklisted domains, rendering purely lexical URL detection insufficient.
    \item \textbf{Pitfalls of Heuristic Max-Pooling:} Simple heuristic max-pooling ($\max(p_t, p_u)$) achieves near-perfect recall (0.9971) but degrades precision to 0.7910, yielding an inferior F1-score of 0.8821 due to excessive false alarms on legitimate URLs.
    \item \textbf{Superiority of Interaction Stacking:} The interaction meta-learner balances precision (0.8936) and recall (0.9693), achieving the highest overall F1-score of \textbf{0.9299} and accuracy of \textbf{88.60\%}.
\end{itemize}

Empirical estimation of the logistic meta-learner parameters in Eq.~\ref{eq:meta_stacking} yields:
\begin{equation}
\beta_0 = -4.3471, \quad \beta_1 = +7.1796, \quad \beta_2 = -1.4680, \quad \beta_3 = +2.1348
\end{equation}
The positive interaction weight $\beta_3 = +2.1348$ demonstrates that when both the text narrative and the embedded hyperlink exhibit suspicious traits, the model applies a super-additive risk escalation.

\subsection{Independent USENIX SOUPS Benchmark Validation}
To evaluate generalizability against unseen external campaign distributions, the frozen ScamGuard model was deployed against the held-out USENIX SOUPS SmishX test suite ($N=39$). ScamGuard correctly identified \textbf{24 out of 25 True Positives} and \textbf{13 out of 14 True Negatives}, demonstrating strong empirical transferability across distinct threat actors.

% ─── Section 6: Qualitative Error Analysis and Discussion ─────────────────────
\section{Qualitative Error Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Security Advisory False Positives}
A key challenge identified during evaluation involves legitimate security advisory notifications issued by financial institutions. Specifically, in sample \texttt{SMISHX-0019}:
\begin{quote}
\emph{``Dear Customer, registration for Google Pay has started for ICICI Bank. If it was not you, report to your bank. Do not share card details/OTP/CVV with anyone.''}
\end{quote}
Although this communication represents legitimate banking advice (Ground Truth label 0), ScamGuard assigns an elevated text threat score of 87/100. Analysis of feature weights indicates that tokens such as \texttt{bank}, \texttt{card}, \texttt{OTP}, \texttt{CVV}, and \texttt{registration} carry high scam coefficients. Because legitimate defensive advisories and social engineering lures utilize identical security vocabulary, lexical linear models struggle to resolve context without deeper semantic comprehension. This highlights the importance of the calibrated $[30, 80)$ uncertainty triage window, which flags such borderline instances for secondary review rather than immediate automated blocking.

\subsection{Hardware Efficiency and Edge Deployment}
ScamGuard executes entirely on commodity CPU architectures without requiring GPU acceleration. Feature extraction and dual-branch inference operate within an average latency of \textbf{42.5 milliseconds} per transaction, enabling real-time inline screening on edge hardware.

% ─── Section 7: Limitations and Future Work ───────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
While ScamGuard provides strong empirical screening with strict leakage prevention, several limitations remain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Cloaking and Redirection:} Static URL analysis cannot detect client-side JavaScript redirects or geo-fenced cloaking payloads without active headless browser rendering.
    \item \textbf{Multilingual Telecommunications:} The current vocabulary is optimized for international English communications; adapting tokenization for tonal and non-Latin alphabets remains an active area of investigation.
    \item \textbf{Client-Side WASM Compilation:} Compiling the Python TF-IDF vectorizer and decision tree structures into client-side WebAssembly (WASM) to facilitate zero-install browser extension screening.
\end{enumerate}

% ─── Section 8: Conclusion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we introduced \textbf{ScamGuard}, a reproducible, lightweight dual-branch screening framework for mobile smishing and phishing hyperlink defense. By combining sublinear word TF-IDF logistic regression with a 13-feature gradient-boosted URL tree and an interaction stacking meta-learner, ScamGuard achieves a 93.82\% accuracy on mobile messages, 92.11\% on phishing URLs, and an F1-score of 0.9299 on real paired messages under rigorous zero-leakage conditions. Operating locally with sub-50ms inference latency, ScamGuard establishes an accessible, privacy-preserving baseline for consumer cyber defense.
